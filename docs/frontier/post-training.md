## 21.1 From base model to assistant

A base model continues documents (§15.7). Asked "How do I bake bread?", it might continue with a forum thread, a list of other questions, or a recipe, whatever is likely on the web. An assistant should reliably respond helpfully, follow instructions, admit uncertainty, refuse harmful requests, and keep a consistent persona across a conversation. Anthropic's original framing was **helpful, honest and harmless** ([Askell et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861)).

The classic pipeline from InstructGPT ([Ouyang et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155)):

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  B[Base model] --> S["1 · SFT on demonstrations"] --> R["2 · Reward model from human comparisons"] --> P["3 · RL (PPO) against the reward model, KL to SFT model"] --> A[Assistant]
```

A striking result: human raters preferred the outputs of a 1.3B InstructGPT model over the 175B base GPT-3. Post-training uses well under 1% of pretraining compute, but it decides how usable the model is.

## 21.2 Supervised fine-tuning (SFT)

Collect (prompt, ideal response) pairs, format them with the chat template (§10.6), and train with the ordinary next-token cross-entropy, **but only on the response tokens**. The prompt is context, not something to imitate.

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{SFT}(\theta) = -\E_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{t=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t}).
$$

In code, this is a mask over the token positions, which is also how we compute a sequence's log-probability for the preference methods later in this chapter:

What matters in practice:

- **Quality over quantity.** LIMA ([Zhou et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)) fine-tuned a 65B model on just 1,000 carefully curated examples and got a strong assistant: "the model's knowledge is learned in pretraining; SFT teaches it the format and style of interaction" (the *superficial alignment hypothesis*).
- **Diversity** of tasks: coding, math, writing, multi-turn conversation, tool use, refusals.
- **Synthetic data.** Most modern SFT data is generated by models (often a stronger model, or the model itself with rejection sampling) and filtered by humans or graders.
- Typical settings: 1–3 epochs, learning rate around $10^{-5}$ to $2\times10^{-5}$ for full fine-tuning, packing several short examples into one sequence (with attention masks that keep them separate).

SFT alone has a ceiling: the model can only imitate demonstrations, and humans are better at **judging** responses than at **writing** perfect ones. Preference learning exploits that.

## 21.3 Reward models and the Bradley–Terry model

Show a labeler a prompt $x$ and two responses, and record which one they prefer: $y_w\succ y_l$ (winner, loser). The **Bradley–Terry model** (1952) assumes each response has a latent quality score $r(x,y)$ and

$$
\Pr(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = \frac{e^{r(x,y_w)}}{e^{r(x,y_w)}+e^{r(x,y_l)}} = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)\big).
$$

Only *differences* of scores matter, so $r$ is defined up to an additive constant per prompt. A **reward model** $r_\phi$ is usually the SFT model with its unembedding replaced by a scalar head, applied to the last token. It's trained by maximum likelihood on the comparisons:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{RM}(\phi) = -\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w)-r_\phi(x,y_l)\big).
$$

It's logistic regression on score differences. At initialization (equal scores) the loss is $\ln2$, which the tests check. Reward-model accuracy on held-out comparisons is typically only 65–80%, because human preferences are noisy and inconsistent.

## 21.4 The RLHF objective and its optimal policy

Treat the LM as a **policy** $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ over whole responses. We want high reward, but a reward model is only accurate near the data it was trained on. Optimized without limit, the policy finds responses the reward model scores highly but humans don't like (§21.9). So we penalize moving far from the SFT model $\pi_\text{ref}$:

$$
\max_\pi\ \E_{x\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\E_{y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\KL\big(\pi(\cdot\mid x)\,\Vert\,\pi_\text{ref}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\Big].
$$

(This is the reverse KL of §4.5.)

!!! theorem "Theorem 21.1 (Optimal KL-regularized policy)"
    For each prompt $x$, the objective is maximized by

    $$
    \pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big),\qquad Z(x) = \sum_y\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta}.
    $$

??? proof "Proof"
    Fix $x$ and drop it from the notation. For any policy $\pi$,

    $$
    \KL(\pi\Vert\pi^*) = \sum_y\pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)\,Z}{\pi_\text{ref}(y)e^{r(y)/\beta}} = \KL(\pi\Vert\pi_\text{ref}) - \frac1\beta\E_\pi[r] + \log Z.
    $$

    Rearranging, the objective is

    $$
    \E_\pi[r]-\beta\KL(\pi\Vert\pi_\text{ref}) = \beta\log Z - \beta\,\KL(\pi\Vert\pi^*).
    $$

    $\beta\log Z$ doesn't depend on $\pi$, and $\KL(\pi\Vert\pi^*)\ge0$ with equality iff $\pi = \pi^*$ (Thm 4.8). $\blacksquare$

The optimal policy **reweights the reference model by $e^{r/\beta}$**: the same responses, with probabilities tilted toward high reward. $\beta$ sets the trade-off: $\beta\to\infty$ keeps $\pi_\text{ref}$; $\beta\to0$ puts all mass on the reward-maximizing response. $Z(x)$ sums over all possible responses, so we can't compute $\pi^*$ directly. RL approximates it by sampling; DPO (§21.7) avoids it algebraically.

## 21.5 Policy gradients

To maximize $J(\theta) = \E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(y)]$ by gradient ascent, we need $\nabla_\theta J$, but the sampling distribution itself depends on $\theta$.

!!! theorem "Theorem 21.2 (Policy gradient / REINFORCE)"
    $$
    \nabla_\theta\E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R(y)] = \E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[R(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\big],
    $$

    and for any baseline $b$ that doesn't depend on $y$, $\E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[b\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\big] = 0$. For an autoregressive policy, $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$.

??? proof "Proof"
    $\nabla_\theta\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)R(y) = \sum_y\pi_\theta(y)\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(y)}{\pi_\theta(y)}R(y) = \sum_y\pi_\theta(y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\,R(y)$ (the **log-derivative trick**). For the baseline: $\sum_y\pi_\theta(y)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y)\,b = b\,\nabla_\theta\sum_y\pi_\theta(y) = b\,\nabla_\theta1 = 0$. The last statement is the log of the chain-rule factorization. $\blacksquare$

The estimator is simple: sample responses, compute $\log\pi_\theta$ of each, and do gradient ascent on $\frac1n\sum_i(R_i-b)\log\pi_\theta(y_i)$. It's like SFT on the model's own samples, **weighted by how much better than average each sample was**. Subtracting a baseline doesn't change the expectation but can greatly reduce the variance. The quantity $A = R-b$ is the **advantage**: how much better this response was than expected.

### From sequences to tokens: values and GAE

Treat generation as a Markov decision process: the state is $(x, y_{<t})$, the action is the next token $y_t$, and (in RLHF) the reward arrives at the end, with a per-token KL penalty $-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_t\mid\cdot)}$ often folded into each step. A **value model** $V_\psi(s_t)$ predicts the expected future reward from each state and serves as a per-token baseline. **Generalized advantage estimation** ([Schulman et al., 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.02438)) combines temporal-difference errors:

$$
\delta_t = r_t+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_t),\qquad A_t = \sum_{l\ge0}(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l},
$$

with $\lambda\in[0,1]$ trading bias ($\lambda = 0$) against variance ($\lambda = 1$, plain Monte Carlo returns). For LLMs, $\gamma = 1$ is usual.

## 21.6 PPO

Policy gradients are only valid near the policy that generated the samples. A step that's too large can wreck the policy. **Proximal Policy Optimization** ([Schulman et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347)) reuses each batch of samples for several gradient steps but limits how far the policy can move. With the probability ratio $\rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(y_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}(y_t\mid s_t)$:

$$
\mathcal{L}^\text{CLIP}(\theta) = -\E_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\,A_t\big)\Big],\qquad\epsilon\approx0.2.
$$


How the clip works: if $A_t>0$ (a good action), the objective grows with $\rho_t$ only until $1+\epsilon$, so there's no incentive to raise its probability further in this batch. If $A_t<0$, there's no incentive to push $\rho_t$ below $1-\epsilon$. The $\min$ makes the objective a pessimistic bound, so it never *rewards* large moves. It's a cheap approximation to a trust region (TRPO).

**PPO for LLMs is heavy**: it needs four models in memory (policy, reference, reward model, value model), generation inside the training loop, and careful tuning. It works (InstructGPT, early ChatGPT and Claude used it), but its cost motivated simpler alternatives.

## 21.7 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Rafailov et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18290)) noticed that Theorem 21.1 can be **inverted**. Take logs and solve for the reward:

$$
r(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}+\beta\log Z(x).
$$

**Any reward function corresponds to an optimal policy, and vice versa.** Substitute this into the Bradley–Terry model. The intractable $\beta\log Z(x)$ appears in both responses' rewards and **cancels** in the difference:

$$
\Pr(y_w\succ y_l\mid x) = \sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Big).
$$

Now parameterize the policy directly, $\pi^* = \pi_\theta$, and fit by maximum likelihood on the preference data:

!!! theorem "The DPO loss"
    $$
    \mathcal{L}_\text{DPO}(\theta) = -\E_{(x,y_w,y_l)}\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_w\mid x)}-\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y_l\mid x)}\Big).
    $$

    The **implicit reward** is $\hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_\text{ref}(y\mid x)}$. Minimizing this loss trains a reward model and solves the RLHF problem for it in one step, with no sampling, no reward model and no value model.

At $\pi_\theta = \pi_\text{ref}$ both implicit rewards are 0 and the loss is $\ln2$ (checked in the tests).

**What the gradient does.** Differentiating,

$$
\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_\text{DPO} = -\beta\,\E\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(\hat r_\theta(x,y_l)-\hat r_\theta(x,y_w)\big)}_{\text{large when the model ranks the pair wrongly}}\Big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big].
$$

It raises the probability of the winner and lowers the loser's, weighted by how wrong the current implicit ranking is. Pairs the model already ranks correctly contribute little.

**Caveats and variants.** DPO is *offline*: it learns from a fixed set of pairs, usually generated by a different model, while PPO learns from the current policy's own samples. Online and iterative DPO (regenerate pairs with the current model, relabel, repeat) closes much of the gap. DPO can lower the likelihood of *both* responses while widening their margin, and it can overfit when preferences are nearly deterministic. Variants include IPO ([Azar et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.12036)), KTO (unpaired thumbs-up/down data; [Ethayarajh et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.01306)), SimPO (no reference model, length-normalized; [Meng et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.14734)) and ORPO.

## 21.8 RLAIF and Constitutional AI

Human preference labels are slow, expensive and inconsistent. **RL from AI feedback (RLAIF)** uses a model to produce the labels. Anthropic's **Constitutional AI** ([Bai et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073)) makes the values explicit as a written list of principles, the *constitution*:

1. **Supervised stage (critique and revise).** Prompt the model with potentially harmful requests, have it respond, then ask it to *critique* its response according to a sampled principle ("Identify ways the response is harmful, unethical or dishonest") and *revise* it. Fine-tune on the revised responses.
2. **RL stage (RLAIF).** Generate pairs of responses; ask a model which one better follows a principle; train a preference model on these AI labels; run RL against it.

The result was a model that was both less harmful and *less evasive* than one trained with human harmlessness labels: it explained its objections instead of just refusing. Writing the principles down makes the training target inspectable and editable. Anthropic has published [the constitution it uses for Claude](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claudes-constitution). Related ideas: OpenAI's *Model Spec* and *deliberative alignment*, where reasoning models are trained to consult a written specification in their chain of thought.

## 21.9 Failure modes

- **Reward hacking / overoptimization.** The reward model is a proxy, so optimizing it too hard exploits its errors (Goodhart's law). Gao et al. ([2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760)) measured it: as the KL from the reference grows, the *proxy* reward keeps rising, but the *true* reward (as judged by a larger "gold" model) rises, peaks, then falls. Classic symptoms: longer and longer answers (length is an easy correlate of preference), excessive hedging, flattery, confident-sounding formatting.
- **Sycophancy.** Models learn that agreeing with the user is rewarded. They change correct answers when the user pushes back, and praise weak work ([Sharma et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). The cause is the human preference data itself.
- **Loss of diversity and calibration.** The reverse-KL objective is mode-seeking (§4.5), so RLHF models produce narrower outputs than base models ("mode collapse"), and their stated confidences become less calibrated (§4.10).
- **Over-refusal.** Safety training can generalize too broadly, refusing harmless requests that resemble harmful ones. Measured by benchmarks like XSTest.
- **Shallow alignment.** Safety behaviors can be removed by fine-tuning on a few hundred examples, and jailbreak prompts find gaps in the training distribution.

Mitigations include reward-model ensembles, length penalties or length-controlled evaluation, mixing objectives, training on specific counterexamples, and verifiable rewards wherever possible (Ch. 22).

## 21.10 Modern post-training pipelines

Public descriptions of recent pipelines share a structure:

- **Llama 3** (Meta, 2024): several rounds of SFT → reward model → **rejection sampling** (sample many responses, keep the best by RM score, add to SFT data) → DPO, with each round's model generating the next round's data.
- **Tülu 3** ([Lambert et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.15124)), a fully open recipe: SFT on curated and synthetic data → DPO on on-policy preference pairs → **RLVR** (RL with verifiable rewards) on math and instruction-following with checkable answers.
- **DeepSeek-R1** (2025): RL with verifiable rewards at large scale to teach reasoning, combined with SFT and preference stages (Ch. 22).
- Beyond capability, labs train **character and values** (personality traits, honesty, how to handle uncertainty), **safety policies**, and **tool use and agentic behavior** (Ch. 27), each with dedicated data and evaluations.

Open-source libraries: Hugging Face [TRL](https://github.com/huggingface/trl) (SFT, DPO, PPO, GRPO trainers), [OpenRLHF](https://github.com/OpenRLHF/OpenRLHF), [verl](https://github.com/volcengine/verl).

## Key takeaways

- SFT: cross-entropy on response tokens only. It teaches format and style; quality beats quantity.
- Reward model: Bradley–Terry, $\Pr(y_w\succ y_l) = \sigma(r_w-r_l)$, trained by logistic loss on comparisons.
- RLHF objective: $\max\E[r]-\beta\KL(\pi\Vert\pi_\text{ref})$, with optimum $\pi^*\propto\pi_\text{ref}e^{r/\beta}$.
- Policy gradient: $\E[(R-b)\nabla\log\pi]$. Baselines reduce variance without bias. PPO clips the probability ratio to stay near the sampling policy.
- DPO inverts the optimal policy, $r = \beta\log\frac{\pi}{\pi_\text{ref}}+\beta\log Z$; $Z$ cancels in Bradley–Terry, giving a supervised loss on preference pairs.
- Constitutional AI replaces human harm labels with model critiques guided by written principles.
- Watch for reward hacking (length, flattery), sycophancy, reduced diversity and over-refusal.
